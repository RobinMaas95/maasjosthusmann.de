## The keyboard that made me slower

The keyboard that made typing more comfortable also made me significantly slower.

When I switched to the MoErgo Glove80, my typing speed dropped. Keys were in unfamiliar places, my fingers had to learn new responsibilities, and even habits I had built over 20 years suddenly worked against me.

That sounds like a terrible trade for a keyboard. Why would anyone deliberately make typing harder?

The answer is that typing speed was not the problem I was trying to solve. I was looking for a setup that would feel better throughout a long working day.

Like a lot of people, I keep looking for small ways to improve my working setup. Some problems are obvious and basically scream at you. Others disappear into familiarity. And for me, the keyboard was one of them.

For someone who spends most of the working day at a computer, it is the device I do almost all my work through. That should have made it an obvious place to look, but its familiarity made it easy to ignore.

I had learned touch typing in elementary school, so typing on a computer always felt natural to me. That early success probably helped me overlook the keyboard for so long. But once I started typing more as a software developer, I noticed that not every keyboard felt the same.

Some keyboards made me produce fewer errors. On others, I could type for longer before feeling fatigue. Those differences started my slow journey into the world of keyboards: different layouts, including a permanent switch from German QWERTZ to English QWERTY, and, of course, different keyboard models.

Eventually, I found one that worked well for me. But finding it was not easy.

## Why the Glove80?

I read dozens of blog posts, reviews, and opinion pieces. I looked at countless product pages, trying to find a keyboard that looked, felt, and sounded right. I regularly work in rooms with other people, so as much as I enjoy a nice mechanical click, I also respect that other people may not enjoy it as much as I do.

Price mattered too. Many split keyboards are a significant investment, especially when it is your first one and you are not even sure whether you will like using one.

In the end, I chose the Glove80. After all these months, I cannot really tell you which detail made the final decision for me. It was probably a combination of favourable reviews, a design I liked, a special-offer period, and a new set of switches that promised a pleasant mechanical feel without a loud clicking sound.

The Glove80 is designed around a few promises that attracted me to split keyboards in the first place. The two separate halves let you position the keyboard so your shoulders can stay relaxed and your forearms and wrists do not have to stretch unnaturally. The keyboard also has several features designed to change how your fingers move:

- **Concave key wells:** The keys curve into a bowl shape, designed to account for different finger lengths and reduce unnecessary travel.
- **Columnar stagger:** The keys follow vertical columns instead of the usual diagonal arrangement, intended to reduce sideways finger movement.
- **Thumb clusters:** Large thumb clusters are designed to move more work to the thumbs and reduce the load on the pinky fingers. Seriously, who decided to overload the weakest finger with so many keys and such wide reaches?

But the same features that promised better ergonomics also changed where my fingers had to move. The ideas behind them are great, but getting used to them takes time. As often, you first have to unlearn old behaviours to enable learning new ones.

And having 20 years of touch typing experiences does not meant that I touch-type every key correctly. There were a few keys, such as `c`, where I somehow learned to used the “wrong” finger.

That worked well enough on the row-staggered keyboards I had used before. With the Glove80’s columnar stagger, those keys no longer felt comfortable when typed with the wrong finger, and I hit the wrong key more often than not. Unlearning these old movements and consistently replacing them with the correct ones definitely took a couple of weeks and was not easy.

The same was true when moving some keys from my pinky fingers to my thumbs. The combination of new finger assignments, the columnar layout, and the unfamiliar curved shape brought my typing speed down significantly at first.

So was the learning curve worth it?

## Eighteen months later

Yes, but not because the Glove80 made me dramatically faster.

About 18 months later, typing on this keyboard feels as natural as typing on any other keyboard I use. Getting used to it also did not “break” me for normal keyboards.

I recently went to Monkeytype and took five 60-second runs on my MacBook keyboard, followed by five runs on the Glove80. The averages were so close that the difference was probably just normal variation between runs: **80.7 words per minute on the MacBook versus 81.3 on the Glove80**. Neither result represents a formal benchmark, but both were reasonably consistent.

The cleaner touch-typing habits carried over to normal keyboards. At the same time, using my pinky fingers more on normal keyboards still feels as natural to me as before.

The more important difference appeared after longer, more intensive typing. The claimed ergonomic advantages of the Glove80 (and of split keyboards more generally) do work for me. My fingers, arms, and shoulders feel better, and I no longer have the feeling that my body needs a break from typing.

And that is the real benefit for me. The Glove80 temporarily made me slower, forced me to correct habits I had built over decades, and asked me to trust a completely unfamiliar layout. But in return, I can work for hours without typing itself getting in the way. My attention stays on the actual challenges of my job instead of the fatigue in my fingers and arms.

For me, that was enough to make the learning curve worthwhile.
